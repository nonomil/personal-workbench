# Free Phonics Materials for Teachers, Tutors, and Families

Literacy is a civil right.

The research supporting phonics is too strong to ignore.

All families deserve access to high-quality phonics materials.

# Why Open Source Phonics?

Many third, fourth, and older-grade students need more practice in order to master the phonics patterns needed to sound out new words and read and write fluently. Commercial programs designed for struggling readers are hundreds of pages long and expensive. Thus, they are not shared with all the teachers, students, and families who could use them.

[Read more](https://www.opensourcephonics.org/why-open-source-phonics/)

# Phonics practice with Open Source Phonics materials is   effective, fun, and hands-on.

![](https://www.opensourcephonics.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/Screen-Shot-2021-07-05-at-1.52.23-PM-1024x674.png)

## [Locking In Patterns](https://www.opensourcephonics.org/sequence/)

Students learn phonics patterns, affixes, and spellings in a step-by-step way.

Oral sound play (which increases phonological awareness) and brainstorming (which increases phonological awareness and vocabulary) is fun. Students get multiple opportunities to read and write new patterns—no guessing involved.

![](https://www.opensourcephonics.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/AdobeStock_409579006-1024x682.jpeg)

## [Writing From Dictation](https://www.opensourcephonics.org/lessons/)

This is where the rubber hits the road. Students “own” the patterns that they can write from dictation. In each lesson, adults read sentences aloud for students to write on white boards. Writing helps reading, as students “lock in” patterns; plus, students expand what they can write and spell with confidence.

![](https://www.opensourcephonics.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/AdobeStock_414390709-1-scaled-e1630096373651-1024x830.jpeg)

## [Reading Aloud](https://www.opensourcephonics.org/decodable-stories/)

Students do “triple reads” of longer decodable stories while adults cheer them on. With repeated readings, students increase fluency, speed, and stamina. Without the “curve balls” of patterns they cannot yet decode, students experience the thrill of fluent reading—where  reading aloud feels more like talking. This practice prepares students to read real texts in school.

# Endorsements for Open Source Phonics

What a service to the world of reading instruction! It's comprehensive, clear, systematic, cumulative, and explicit. The lesson format is excellent: review, phoneme awareness, concept presentation, guided practice in both reading and writing to dictation. The scope and sequence is solid, as good as any phonics program out there. The quality and amount of decodable text practice is exceptional!! The selection, inclusion, and gradual teaching of more irregular words is very well done and the methods align with current understandings.

Dr. Louisa MoatsLiteracy Expert and Author of LETRS

I whole-heartedly support initiatives such as this one which work to provide high quality literacy instructional materials to all teachers and readers. Decodable texts provide students the opportunity to apply the phonics patterns they have been taught in decoding words in context. Using context clues to “guess” unknown words and/or skipping unknown words are

unreliable strategies that are not supported by research.

Skilled readers are able to use their knowledge of phonics (e.g., sound-symbol correspondences and patterns, orthographic syllable types, morphology, and syllable division patterns) to decode unknown words, rather than guess or skip. Decodable texts contain words with phonics patterns students have been taught so they can learn to utilize these skills proficiently, and over time move to less controlled and more authentic texts. I admire and applaud Katie Spurlock’s work to make decodable texts available to every teacher and reader with internet access. I have reviewed her phonics sequence, lessons, and initial set of decodables and confirm their alignment with evidence-based structured literacy instruction.

**Every** child deserves the best opportunity to learn to read, and this is one step in that direction.


EMILY CANTRELL Clinical Associate ProfessorTeaching, Learning, and Culture Texas A&M University
